# Carry On? — A Status Update

*From the Ledge | May 2026*

---

In January, I drew a line: Takaichi's unfunded investment spree would not produce a bond market crisis — the BoJ owns too much of the JGB market for that — but *would* trigger a yen currency crisis. The mechanism I outlined was the carry trade unwind: MoF/BoJ FX intervention would spook global equity markets by forcing a sudden reversal of the endemic yen carry. PE funds invested in Japan, and anyone levered to the divergence between near-zero Japanese rates and the rest of the world, would be collateral damage.

Four months later, the BoJ has spent roughly ¥8.5 trillion in eight days defending the yen. The efficacy of each successive intervention has declined. Takaichi's approval rating has fallen from 79.5% to 56%. This is not a crisis — yet. But it is the beginning of one, and the shape is familiar.

---

## The 2006/07 Analogy

I have written before about why this setup rhymes with 2006/07, when the last great yen carry trade began to unwind. The difference then was simpler architecture: fewer instruments, more transparent leverage, a financial system that was already under stress for other reasons. The carry trade was the lit match, not the petrol.

Today the architecture is more complex. Global PE funds have borrowed cheap yen — sometimes indirectly, through synthetic structures — to invest in Japanese assets. Family offices have been running the classic dollar-asset/yen-liability spread. Hedge funds are leveraged against it both ways.

What the BoJ is discovering is that it has a playbook for *one of these* problems, not for all of them at once.

The declining efficacy of intervention is the tell. Each successive tranche is moving the yen less than the last. The market is testing the MoF's will, and the MoF is blinking.

## Three Scenarios

I give conviction weights here in the same spirit as the ledger: these are positions on the *regime*, not predictions of the exact level or date.

---

### 1. Interventions Fade, Slow Crack — Base Case

The BoJ continues to intervene with declining effect. The yen stabilises in a wide range but the direction of travel remains weakening. Takaichi's fiscal programme loses credibility but not coalition support — she survives 2026, and the full unwind does not materialise this year.

Private credit stress in Japan-exposed PE portfolios shows up in quarterly marks but does not become a headline event. The story becomes a slow burn: the regime has shifted — intervention is no longer the circuit-breaker it was — but the acute phase waits for a trigger that does not arrive on this timeline.

**Conviction: ★** — consistent with the January call being directionally correct, timeline extended.

---

### 2. Coalition Wobble, Full Unwind — Tail Risk

A political event — loss of a key coalition partner, a snap election scare, or a decisive defeat in an Upper House by-election — forces a credibility crisis. The BoJ is caught between supporting the yen and keeping JGB yields from spiking; it cannot do both indefinitely at current fiscal trajectories.

A disorderly yen weakening through ¥165 triggers margin calls on carry positions and a partial re-run of August 2024. Global equity markets register the contagion. The Nikkei falls sharply. Dollar-yen volatility spikes. The Fed is dragged in diplomatically if not operationally.

This is the full version of the January call playing out. It is a tail risk because the sequencing requires multiple things to go wrong simultaneously — but none of those things are individually unlikely. Watch the coalition arithmetic: that is where this breaks, if it breaks.

**Conviction: ★★** *if* a coalition wobble materialises. Currently not the base, but the watch item.

---

### 3. Hormuz De-escalation, Yen Stabilises in 150s — Contrarian Read

A reduction in Hormuz risk — US-Iran back-channel, Saudi mediation — cuts the oil price materially. This reduces Japan's import bill substantially and takes pressure off the current account. Simultaneously, the Fed signals a cut cycle, compressing the USD/JPY spread from the dollar side rather than the yen side.

The yen stabilises in the 150–154 range without a disorderly intervention cycle. Takaichi's approval holds in the low 50s — uncomfortable but survivable. The coalition muddies through.

This is the "rate divergence was always the story, and it's closing from the dollar side" outcome. I take it seriously not because it is the most likely path, but because the market is currently priced for the tail, and a sharp Hormuz premium unwind would change the yen thesis materially by September.

**Conviction: ★** — worth holding against the other two. Not the base case.

## What the Ledger Says

The BoJ interventions are a feature of the regime shift, not a refutation of it. Declining efficacy is not a sign that the call was wrong; it is a sign that the call is running on schedule.

The line I drew in January was structural. What the BoJ is now drawing in dollars is the same line — in real time, at great expense, with diminishing returns. They are running out of ink.
